1. Problem Framing & Audience

Food delivery apps are high-frequency, time-pressured interfaces with dense data (menus, allergens, fees, live tracking). Conventional designs often create visual clutter and decision fatigue.

Design goal: Organise information according to how the human visual system naturally groups stimuli (Gestalt laws) so users can scan and decide faster with fewer errors.

Target users

  • Busy professionals — need rapid scannability, clear pricing, one-tap dietary filters during short breaks.
  • Families & health-conscious users — need reliable allergen cues, customisable spice levels, and trustworthy live tracking.

Design Process, Evidence & Limitations

The following wireframes are included as design artefacts for this submission. They communicate the intended hierarchy and flow; they are not presented as photographs of hand-drawn sketches or proof of user testing.

1. Problem statement

Food-delivery interfaces can overload users with menu choices, prices, delivery details and customisation decisions. CraveWave organises those elements into predictable groups and highlights the next action so a user can move from discovery to order tracking with less visual effort.

Target audience and user needs

  • People ordering during short breaks: quick scanning, clear prices and a short path to checkout.
  • People comparing dishes: consistent cuisine labels, readable descriptions and visible dietary tags.
  • People customising an order: focused choices and clear confirmation feedback.

2. Audience snapshot & task journey

Primary persona · The time-conscious student

Context: ordering between classes or after a long day, often on a phone.

  • Needs prices and delivery expectations visible before choosing.
  • Wants quick vegetarian, non-vegetarian and dietary filtering.
  • Needs a simple cart and clear confirmation before placing a demo order.

Design response

Keep discovery, comparison and ordering in one clear hierarchy. Use repeated card patterns, grouped metadata, visible totals and a focused checkout step to reduce unnecessary searching.

Scope note: this persona is a design assumption for the prototype, not a participant profile from completed research.

Discover→Choose→Customise→Review→Checkout→Track

7. Functional scope and limitations

  • Working in this prototype: live menu search, cuisine and dietary filtering, price/rating/name sorting, favourites, cart quantity updates, local cart persistence, validated demo checkout, generated demo order reference and animated tracking.
  • Simulated: payment and order placement; no real payment, restaurant, delivery partner or backend service is connected.
  • Simulated: tracker animation is deterministic and does not use GPS or live courier data.
  • Static: menu items and prices are hard-coded sample content; they are not live restaurant offers.
  • Not yet implemented: user accounts, server-side order storage, real payment processing, restaurant inventory, courier GPS, verified allergen data, multilingual content and dark mode. Checkout form validation is client-side only.

8. Accessibility and testing checklist

  • Semantic header, navigation, main and section landmarks.
  • Skip link and visible keyboard focus indicators.
  • Native cursor retained; controls remain keyboard operable.
  • Live status region announces menu result updates.
  • Reduced-motion preferences are respected by decorative animation.
  • Responsive layout rules are included, but must still be checked on real devices and browsers.

Before submission, manually test: keyboard-only navigation, screen-reader labels, zoom at 200%, contrast with a checker, narrow mobile viewport, menu overflow, modal close behaviour, cart drawer focus, and every CTA.
